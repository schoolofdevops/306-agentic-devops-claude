Kubernetes and GitOps

The previous module had an agent write Terraform against a brief. This one points the same method at the other half of the platform: the chart that decides how your service reaches a cluster, and the path that change has to travel to get there.

Here is the job. Northstar's campaign opens in nine days. The team ships several times a day during a campaign, and last month a routine deploy of orders-api took every pod out at once — no bad command, no failed build, just a chart that was written that way. You are going to have Claude fix it: you write the brief, the platform-engineer role does the design under the superpowers harness, and the tests it writes are watched failing before they pass. Then the change goes down the golden path — Git, the reconciler, the cluster — and you measure what you built.

By the end you will have a chart you could defend in a change review, and a number you took yourself: how many pods keep serving through a deploy, next to what the same deploy does without the fix.

The render is the artifact, not the template​

A Helm template is a form letter. The chart is the letter with blanks, the values file is the mail-merge list, and what actually gets posted is neither of them — it is the merged result. Reviewing the template and approving the change is proofreading a form letter without ever looking at an envelope.

That is why every instruction in this module — the agent's, the tests', yours — reads helm template output rather than the files it came from. A value can be perfectly correct in values.yaml and never reach the manifest because no template references it. The render is the only artifact Kubernetes ever sees.

It also explains the most common false comfort in chart work. helm lint reads the form of the chart: is the YAML valid, are the required fields present, does it install. On the chart you are about to fix, helm lint reports zero charts failed — while the chart guarantees an outage on every deploy. Lint has never meant safe. It means installable.

A serving floor is arithmetic, not a setting​

When Kubernetes rolls out a new version, two numbers decide whether anybody notices: maxSurge, how many extra pods it may create, and maxUnavailable, how many existing pods it may take away before replacements are ready.

Northstar's chart shipped with maxUnavailable: "100%" and replicaCount: 1. Read them together, because separately each one looks defensible:

  • 100% permits the rollout to remove every serving pod before a single replacement is Ready.
  • With one replica, there was never a second pod to carry the traffic anyway.

Fixing either number alone changes nothing. A 25% ceiling on a single replica still rounds down to taking the only pod away. Two replicas with a 100% ceiling still allows both to go at once. The floor is a relationship between the two values, which is exactly the kind of property a written standard catches and a code review does not, because the two numbers usually sit in different parts of the file.

In the lab you measure this rather than trusting it: a sampler counts available pods twice a second while a real change goes through Git. With the floor in place the count never drops below two. With the original values, it sits at zero for about nine and a half seconds — on an ordinary version bump, with nothing failing and every dashboard green thirty seconds later.

The brief is yours; the design is not​

The habit that makes agentic chart work reliable is the same one from the IaC module: you write down what you want before you ask for it.

For this module that is a short SPEC.md inside the chart directory — why the change is needed, what it must achieve, what is not negotiable, and what "done" means. Eleven lines is enough. Notice what does not go in it: no Kubernetes field names. maxUnavailable, runAsNonRoot and the rest live in the house standard, and repeating them in the brief both duplicates a thing you must now keep in sync and pre-empts the design you are asking for.

It is also not a CLAUDE.md. That distinction is practical: a CLAUDE.md is injected into every turn of every session that runs in its directory, which is right for rules that are always true and wrong for a build brief. Put the brief there and, months after the chart ships, every session in that folder still pays to be told about finished work.

The house standard is a skill, not a habit​

Handing test-driven development to an agent only works if there is something to hold it to. Northstar's helm-style skill is that something, and three parts of it earn their place:

  • The baselines that are hardcoded rather than variabilized. Every container declares requests and limits. Every pod runs non-root. A rolling update always leaves a floor. These are written into the template, not exposed as values — because a guarantee a values file can switch off during an incident was never a guarantee.
  • A vocabulary table of near misses. 256M is not 256Mi. cpu: 100 is a hundred cores, not a hundred millicores. runtime/default is the annotation-era spelling that does nothing as a field value. Every one of these renders, lints, and then behaves wrongly — which is precisely why they belong in a file rather than in somebody's memory.
  • The render-test contract. Tests live next to the chart, assert on rendered output, and report the value they found when they fail.

One entry in that table is worth reading twice, because it is where charts and containers actually meet. runAsNonRoot: true on its own is not enough: the kubelet has to verify the user is not root before the container starts, and an image whose USER is a name rather than a number gives it nothing to check. The pod fails at container creation with a configuration error, long after lint, render and review all said yes. A numeric runAsUser alongside it is what makes the baseline real.

Build it test-first, in a worktree you did not create​

With the brief written, the work runs the same way any serious change does:

  1. Launch Claude as the role: claude --agent platform-engineer. That starts the session with the role's system prompt, model and tool list. It can write under platform/helm/, it can run helm template and helm lint and read-only kubectl, and it has no path to kubectl apply or helm upgrade at all. The boundary is real from the first keystroke.
  2. Ask for isolation and check you got it. The using-git-worktrees skill creates the worktree, checks whether you are already isolated first, and carries uncommitted work across. It is also the least predictable step in the module, so git worktree list is part of the step rather than an afterthought. The directory name is the agent's choice — on the captured run it picked harden-orders-api-campaign — which is why you read the path off the command instead of assuming one.
  3. Hand over the brief and let the harness work. Brainstorming fires before any code and stops to put a design in front of you. Then a plan. Then the test-first cycle.
  4. Watch the tests fail. This is the step people skip, and skipping it is how a suite of assertions that can never fail ends up in a repository.

One detail decides whether any of that happens: a role can only invoke a skill if Skill is in its tools list. Leave it out and the agent still finds the standard and reads it as an ordinary file, still edits the chart, still sounds right — and the brainstorm, the plan and the test-first discipline never run. You get the output without the method, and nothing in the transcript says so.

What came back on the captured run was three tests and a runner, written against the unmodified chart and reported honestly:

FAIL: req_cpu is unset — container is BestEffort
FAIL: maxUnavailable=100% takes every pod down at once
FAIL: securityContext.runAsNonRoot is 'null', want true

PASS 0 / FAIL 3

Then the chart changed and the same command said PASS 3 / FAIL 0. You wrote eleven lines of requirements and no field names; what you got back was sixty-one lines of tests holding twelve lines of values in place. That ratio is the point — the fix is small and obvious, and the reason it is still correct in six months is the part that took the work.

The golden path: the reconciler is the only writer​

Once the chart is right, it still has to reach a cluster, and the way it travels is the other half of this module.

The dotted line is the whole design. The agent writes desired state and stops. A reconciler — Argo CD here — is the single identity that turns merged Git into cluster state. Every change to the running system therefore has a commit, an author and a review behind it, because there is no other way in.

Three separate controls hold that line, and the useful thing about them is that they fail in three different ways:

ControlWhat it doesWhat it does not do
selfHeal on the reconcilerNotices the cluster no longer matches Git and puts it backPrevent the change. A manual kubectl scale succeeds, and runs unexplained until the reconciler catches up
The PreToolUse change gateRefuses specific commands before they execute — namespace deletes, wildcard mutations, docker pushProtect anything nobody enumerated. A new namespace is outside the rule until someone adds it
The read-only identityThe API server refuses every write verb, whatever the agent intendsCare about intent at all — which is its strength

Only the third is enforcement in the strict sense. The first is reversal, and the gap between a manual change and the reconciler noticing it is a window where production is running something no commit explains.

The gate's docker push denial is worth pausing on, because it is where the two change paths separate. Desired state travels through Git to the reconciler; images travel through CI. They are different artifacts with different review paths, and a chart rollback does not roll back an image. That matters more than it sounds: an image tagged latest has no prior artifact to roll back to, which makes it precisely the wrong tag to be carrying into a campaign.

Authority is about the change, not the file​

Before anything merges, an independent reviewer looks at it. change-reviewer runs on Opus, is read-only by construction — no Edit or Write tool at all — and judges five things: does the change match its stated intent, is the blast radius acceptable, is there a rollback path, are there secrets in the diff, and was every change in it one its author was allowed to make.

In the lab you plant something for it to find: a service-account annotation binding the pod to an AWS admin role, dropped into the values file inside a change about rollout safety. The reviewer blocks it, and the reason it gives is the sharpest idea in this module:

platform-engineer has approve: [] — the file path is writable, the change class is not self-approvable. Path permission ≠ change-class authority.

The role is allowed to edit that file. It is not allowed to make that kind of change. No file-path permission model can tell those apart, which is why an authority matrix exists alongside the tool list — and why permission changes are so effective at riding into production inside a pull request about something else.

It also found three things nobody planted, including the latest tag having no rollback target. A reviewer that only reports what you pointed it at is a reviewer you have to aim correctly every time.

Match the model to the job​

The captured build cost $0.81 on Sonnet across two turns — design, plan, failing tests, chart edits, verification. The independent review cost $0.45 on Opus, and returned a block that separated an approved commit from a smuggled privilege change.

Everything else in the module costs nothing at all: the renders, the lint, the tests, the rollout sampler, the gate checks, every kubectl call. That is the pattern worth carrying to work — the deterministic half of a review belongs in a script, because a script does not need a model, does not vary between runs, and can fail a pipeline. Spend the model on the judgment: intent, blast radius, authority, rollback. Then let it read the script's output instead of re-deriving it.

What the controls miss​

Every control in this module has an edge, and three of them are places where everything you built reports success.

baseline does not mean non-root. The northstar namespace enforces the baseline Pod Security Standard and only warns about restricted. In the lab you start a plain root pod in that namespace and it is admitted — with a warning that scrolls past in CI output. So the non-root property of your service comes from your chart and from nowhere else. Admission would not have caught its absence.

A change can pass every offline control and never apply. Adding a label to the chart's selector is about the safest-looking edit there is. Lint passes, the tests pass, the render is correct — and the API server rejects it forever, because spec.selector is immutable after creation. The reconciler reports OutOfSync while the health column still says Healthy, since the service is happily running the old spec. A reviewed, merged, approved change that silently never lands is a worse failure than one that breaks loudly.

Your tests see one render. Chart tests render the chart with a particular set of values — the ones this Application uses. Put the same defect in a production overlay instead and the suite still reports PASS 3 / FAIL 0 while the production render takes every pod down at once. What your tests can see is what you asserted on.

And one thing that turns out not to be a limit, because it was measured rather than repeated: local kind clusters are widely said to ignore NetworkPolicy. On the version this course is written against, a deny-all policy is enforced and the traffic stops. The lab runs the check both ways. Whether your CNI enforces policy is a fact about the cluster you are running today, and it is one command to find out — which is a better habit than either belief.

What you will do in the lab​

  1. Read the chart as the cluster will receive it — render it, find the three defects, and watch helm lint pass on all of them.
  2. Ask the harness for an isolated workspace, then verify you actually got one.
  3. Write the brief — requirements, not design, and not a CLAUDE.md.
  4. Hand it to the platform-engineer role and let superpowers brainstorm, plan and work test-first.
  5. Watch three tests fail against the current chart, then pass against the fixed one.
  6. Verify every claim yourself, including the ratio of tests to values in the diff.
  7. Get an independent verdict with an IAM binding planted in the branch, and see authority checked as a change class rather than a file path.
  8. Merge, push, and put the service on the golden path through the local remote and the reconciler.
  9. Measure the serving floor during a real rollout — then take it away and measure the outage.
  10. Prove the three cluster controls, and notice that one of them reverts rather than prevents.
  11. Find the edges: a root pod admitted by baseline, a change that can never apply, and a defect your tests cannot see.

The Deep Dive takes the other half of the story: a rollout that fails in production, and how to tell the difference between an unhealthy application, a wrong probe, a stuck rollout and a routing problem — with the repair going through Git, because that is the only way in.
