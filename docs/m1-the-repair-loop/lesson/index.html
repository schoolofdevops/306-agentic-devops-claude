Introduction to Agentic DevOps

You have just cloned Northstar Commerce and run preflight. The tools are green. Something in the platform is broken, and in the lab you will hand it straight to Claude and watch it get fixed in about fifteen minutes.

Then you will watch the same agent, in the same session, produce a change that looks correct, passes every check you would run by eye, and is wrong.

That second outcome is why this module exists. Getting an agent to fix a bug is easy and you will do it almost immediately. Knowing when it did not is the skill, and it is the difference between an engineer who uses AI safely and one who ships whatever the model suggested and hopes.

Why this loop, and why now​

Think about the last time a teammate handed you a "quick fix" that turned into an afternoon. The fix itself was small. What cost you the afternoon was everything around it: figuring out what was actually wrong, making sure the change didn't touch anything it shouldn't, and confirming afterward that the symptom was gone and nothing else broke.

An AI coding agent makes the middle step (writing the change) nearly free. That is exactly why the steps on either side matter more, not less. When producing a diff costs seconds, the bottleneck moves to diagnosis (are we fixing the right thing?) and verification (did it actually work?). An agent that can generate ten plausible fixes a minute is a liability unless you have a discipline that says which one is correct and how you know.

This module isn't about learning to prompt an AI to fix a bug. It's about running a repeatable loop where the agent does the mechanical work while you stay accountable for the direction and the proof.

Analogy: the technician's checklist versus the tinkerer​

Imagine two people fixing a car that won't start.

The tinkerer pops the hood and starts swapping parts. New battery: still dead. New starter: still dead. Three hours later the car runs, but nobody knows which change fixed it, and two of the "fixes" are now just extra risk sitting in the engine bay.

The technician works a checklist. Read the fault code. Reproduce the symptom. Form one hypothesis. Change one thing. Confirm the symptom is gone. When the car starts, they know why it starts, and the repair record proves it.

Agentic DevOps is the technician's method applied to systems, with an AI holding the wrench. The AI is fast and tireless, but a fast tinkerer is more dangerous than a slow one. The checklist is what keeps speed from turning into chaos.

The diagnose → change → verify loop​

The loop is the how, and it does not change when an agent runs it. What changes is who performs each step and how you verify the result — which is why the lab has you delegate the whole loop immediately and then spend the rest of the module learning to check its work.

Notice the loop, not the line. When verification comes back red, you do not pile a second change on top of the first. You go back to diagnosis. A red grader means your hypothesis was wrong or incomplete, and stacking fixes on a wrong hypothesis is exactly the tinkerer's trap.

1. Bounded evidence collection​

Analogy: a good detective seals off the room before dusting for prints. They don't let the whole precinct trample through. Every extra footprint is noise that hides the one print that matters.

Before you ask anyone, human or agent, to diagnose a problem, you decide what evidence they get to see. This is bounded evidence: you scope the inputs to the smallest set that could contain the answer.

Why bound it? Two reasons that show up in your work every day:

  • Signal. A diagnosis made from the readiness endpoint's output plus the one file that serves it is sharper than a diagnosis made from the entire repository. More context is not more insight; past a point it is just more places to be wrong.
  • Cost and blast radius. When you later let an agent read the evidence, the tokens you feed it and the files you expose are both a budget and a risk surface. An agent that has read only health.py cannot confidently propose editing your Terraform. Scoping the input scopes the possible output.

In this lab you collect exactly two pieces of evidence: the live output of the broken endpoint, and the single file that produces it. That is enough to diagnose the bug with certainty, and no more than enough.

Bounded evidence is a habit, not a restriction

Bounding evidence is not about hiding things from the agent. It is about giving it a clean, relevant working set so its reasoning, and yours, stays anchored to what actually matters.

2. Branch-only changes​

Analogy: a surgeon operates in a theatre, not in the hospital lobby. The theatre is isolated on purpose: if something goes wrong, it is contained, and the rest of the hospital keeps running.

You never repair on main directly. Every change lands on a branch first. On a branch you can review the full diff, run the grader, throw the whole thing away with one command when the hypothesis was wrong, and merge only once verification is green.

This matters even more with an agent in the loop. An agent editing files directly on main gives you a changed system and no clean record of what it touched. A branch turns every agent change into a reviewable, revertable proposal. The branch is the boundary between "the agent did something" and "I approved something the agent did."

3. Deterministic verification: the grader​

Analogy: a lab test result is a number against a reference range, not the nurse saying "you look healthy." "Looks healthy" is a vibe. "Glucose 92 mg/dL, range 70–100" is a fact you can act on.

Here is the most important idea in this module: "it seems to work" is not verification. A change is verified when a deterministic check, the same inputs producing the same pass/fail every time, says so.

Northstar ships a grader for exactly this:

./scripts/grade.sh m1

It does not ask your opinion. It curls the real endpoints, inspects the actual code, checks the Helm probe path, and prints PASS or FAIL with a count. Run it before your fix and it is red. Run it after and it turns green. That transition, red to green, on demand, reproducibly, is what "verified" means for the rest of this course.

Vibes-based verification fails in two directions, and an agent amplifies both. It gives false confidence (the agent says "Fixed!" while the symptom is still there under a slightly different request) and false alarm (you distrust a change that actually worked). A deterministic grader takes the agent's opinion, and yours, out of the question of whether the job is done.

The autonomy ladder: matching trust to risk​

The loop above is the how. The last idea in this module is who drives, and that changes with the task.

Analogy: driver assistance in a car. Cruise control holds your speed but you steer. Lane-keeping nudges the wheel but you supervise. Full self-driving takes the whole trip. You would use cruise control on an empty highway and take full manual control in a crowded parking lot. The right level depends on how much can go wrong, and how fast.

The same ladder applies to DevOps work:

LevelWho decidesWho actsGood fit
ManualHumanHumanIrreversible, high-stakes, or novel work. You write the Terraform, the Helm chart, the runbook.
AI AugmentedHumanHuman, Claude assistsClaude Code suggests edits, skills encode repeatable procedures, MCP tools connect external data. You drive; the AI accelerates.
AgenticAgent (with varying human oversight)AgentThe agent has its own agency. At the semi-autonomous end, it investigates and recommends. You approve each action. At the autonomous end, it detects, acts, and verifies within guardrails with no human gate. The dial between these two ends is how much you trust the verification.

The choice is a risk decision, not a capability decision. The question is never "can the agent do this?" It is "if the agent gets this wrong, how bad is it, how fast will I know, and how easily can I undo it?" A one-line probe fix on a branch with a deterministic grader is a safe candidate for agentic work: the diff is small, the grader is trustworthy, and git makes it revertable. A production database migration at peak traffic is not, no matter how capable the agent is.

Semi-autonomous and autonomous are two points on the same spectrum. The real jump happens earlier, between AI as a tool you wield (augmented) and AI as an agent that acts (agentic). Once the agent has agency, the question is simply how far you let it run before it checks in with you.

As you progress through this course, you will climb the ladder:

  • Module 1 (Manual): you run the full repair loop yourself, no agent
  • Modules 2–4 (AI Augmented): Claude assists your diagnosis and edits, you drive every decision
  • Modules 5–19 (Agentic): skills, subagents, orchestration topologies, and governed workflows where the agent has increasing agency, from proposing changes you approve to detecting and resolving issues within guardrails

You will do this module's fix fully manually so you understand every step. In later modules you hand more of the loop to the agent, but only as far up this ladder as the task's risk allows. The loop stays the same at every level; what changes is how much of it you drive yourself.

What you'll do in the lab​

You will delegate a real defect and then test whether you can tell good agent work from bad. Northstar's orders-api reports itself "not ready" because its readiness check probes the wrong path on inventory-api.

You will start the platform, hand the bug to Claude and approve your first permission prompt, watch it find a second surface of the same bug you did not know about, and then ask it for a reasonable-sounding improvement that quietly removes the safety property. curl will say the service is healthy. The grader will disagree, and the grader will be right.

Then you will write down the context that made a governed agent behave differently from an ungoverned one — the first file in the operating layer this course spends nineteen modules building.
