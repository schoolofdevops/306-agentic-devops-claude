SRE, Observability & Incident Response

Every module so far taught the agent to change the estate safely — Terraform plans, Helm renders, CI gates, the reconciler as the only writer. This module is about the other half of the job: what happens when something is already wrong, the pager is going off, and someone has to decide what to look at first. That "someone" is increasingly an agent working alongside you, and the failure mode is specific and expensive. An agent handed an incident and a metrics stack will do exactly what an inexperienced responder does — pull everything, correlate everything, and arrive forty minutes later with a confident story that happens to be wrong. Observability without discipline is not evidence; it is a firehose pointed at your context window.

Here is the situation this module closes. Northstar's orders-api starts throwing errors during a busy period. p95 latency climbs from under 200ms to 28 seconds. The error rate crosses 30%. Two Prometheus alerts are firing. The ops dashboard is a wall of red sparklines. You — or the agent you dispatch — have to answer three questions in order: how far outside normal are we (against what objective?), what is the evidence (bounded, not everything), and what changed (the causal event, not a plausible-sounding guess). Get the order wrong and you spend the incident window scaling up CPU for a problem that was never about CPU. You are going to build the machinery that answers those three questions with discipline, and then run the incident it was built for.

The SLO is the speed limit, and the error budget is the fine you can afford​

Before any dashboard, before any query, there is one number that turns "the site feels slow" into a decision you can defend: the Service Level Objective.

Analogy: an SLO is a speed limit on a road, and the error budget is how much you are allowed to speed before you lose your license. A speed limit is not a description of how fast cars go — it is a commitment about what is acceptable, set deliberately, that everyone can check their behavior against. Without it, "you were going too fast" is an argument; with it, "you were doing 90 in a 65" is a fact. The error budget is the twist that makes it usable: you are not required to drive at exactly the limit at all times. You have a margin — a few percent over a rolling window — and as long as you stay inside it, you are fine. Burn the whole margin in one reckless afternoon and the consequence is automatic, not a judgment call. That is the shift SLOs make: reliability stops being a feeling ("the service seems healthy") and becomes an accounting question ("how much of this month's budget have we spent, and how fast are we spending it right now").

For orders-api, Northstar commits to two objectives, and they are written down as real Prometheus rules, not aspirations in a wiki:

  • Availability: error rate below 1% over a rolling 5-minute window. The error budget is the 1% you are allowed to fail. If you serve 100,000 requests, up to 1,000 may be 5xx before you have breached.
  • Latency: p95 request duration below 500ms. Ninety-five percent of requests finish inside half a second; the slow 5% is your budget.

The reason to encode these as recording rules and alerts — rather than eyeballing a graph — is that it makes the objective computable. An alert can fire on "error rate exceeds 1% for 5 minutes" because orders_api:error_rate:5m > 0.01 is a query a machine evaluates continuously. And critically, it separates two things responders constantly conflate: the SLO breach (you crossed the line you promised not to cross) from the page (the thing severe enough to wake someone). Northstar has both — a warning-severity SLOErrorBudgetBurn at 1%, and a separate critical HighErrorRate at 5% that routes as a real page. The budget tells you how bad; the page tells you drop what you are doing.

Why this matters for agentic work

An agent asked "is orders-api healthy?" with no SLO has to invent a threshold, and it will pick a plausible one that is not yours. An agent asked "is orders-api inside its error budget?" has a named number to evaluate against — 0.01 for error rate, 0.5 seconds for p95 — and its answer is checkable. SLOs turn a subjective health question into a deterministic one, which is exactly the kind of question you can safely delegate.

The golden signals are the four gauges you read first​

An incident throws hundreds of possible metrics at you. The discipline is to not look at hundreds. There is a small, fixed set that answers "is this service in trouble and why," and everything else is a follow-up you earn by reading these first.

Analogy: the golden signals are the four gauges on a car dashboard. A modern car exposes thousands of sensor values over its diagnostic bus — but the dashboard shows you four things while you drive: speed, fuel, engine temperature, and the oil light. Those four are not arbitrary; they are the minimal set that tells you whether to keep driving, slow down, or pull over now. You do not read the mass-airflow sensor to notice the engine is overheating — you read the temperature gauge, and only if it is pinned do you pop the hood for the deeper reading. Observability works the same way. There are four signals that, read first, tell you where the trouble is:

SignalThe question it answersThe orders-api metric
LatencyHow long are requests taking?orders_api_request_duration_seconds (histogram → p95)
TrafficHow much demand is arriving?orders_api_requests_total (rate)
ErrorsWhat fraction is failing?orders_api_requests_total{status=~"5.."} / total
SaturationHow full is the system?CPU/memory pressure, queue depth, orders_api_upstream_retries_total climbing

These four are the bounded evidence set. When the pager fires, the first move is not "show me all metrics" — it is "read the four gauges." Latency spiking with errors climbing and traffic flat says the service is failing under normal load — something internal broke. Latency and errors climbing with traffic says you are overwhelmed. Saturation pinned with the others calm says you are about to fall over but have not yet. The pattern across the four gauges points you at a class of cause before you have run a single deep query. That is the whole value: four reads, and you know which hood to pop.

Metrics, logs, events, and traces are evidence — with different jobs​

The four golden signals are metrics, but an incident needs more than metrics, and the mistake is treating all telemetry as interchangeable. Each of the four telemetry types answers a different question, and knowing which to reach for is the difference between bounded investigation and a firehose.

Analogy: think of a doctor diagnosing a patient. Metrics are the vital signs — heart rate, blood pressure, temperature — cheap to take continuously, perfect for is something wrong and how wrong, useless for why. Logs are the patient's own account — "it started after lunch, sharp pain here" — rich detail about specific events, but you cannot read every word the patient ever spoke, so you ask targeted questions. Events are the chart annotations — "administered medication at 14:30," "surgery on the 12th" — the record of what was done and when, which is how you correlate a symptom with a change. Traces are the full path of one blood sample through the lab — this request went to the API, which called inventory, which timed out, which triggered a retry — the end-to-end story of a single transaction. A good doctor reads vitals first (metrics), then asks pointed questions (logs), checks what changed recently (events), and orders a full workup on one representative case (traces) — in that order, escalating from cheap-and-broad to expensive-and-narrow.

The failure the agent must avoid is inverting that order — starting with "grep all the logs" or "trace everything," which is expensive, unbounded, and buries the signal. The right sequence is:

Metrics tell you that and how much. Events tell you what changed. Logs and traces tell you why, on the narrow slice the metrics and events already pointed you at. Reach for the expensive tools last, aimed at the smallest slice — that is evidence discipline, and it is the same "read the effect, not everything" instinct you have carried since the Terraform plan review.

Anti-firehose: a bounded query is a question, not a dump​

This is the discipline the whole module rests on, and it is where an agent most needs a leash. "Show me everything" is not a query — it is an abdication. A bounded query has three limits baked in, and every observability question the agent asks should carry all three.

Analogy: a bounded query is a search warrant, and "show me everything" is a fishing expedition. A warrant names a place, a time, and a thing to look for — "the garage, on the night of the 3rd, for a red toolbox." It is specific because specificity is what makes the search legitimate and fast: the officer knows when they are done. A fishing expedition — "let us look through everything this person owns and see what turns up" — is slow, produces mountains of irrelevant material, and is exactly how you end up with a confident conclusion built on a coincidence. Unbounded observability queries fail the same way: they return so much that the responder (or the agent's context window) drowns, and the noise-to-signal ratio guarantees a plausible-but-wrong correlation.

The three bounds every query must carry:

  • Time window. [5m], not "all history." The Northstar SLI queries use rate(...[5m]) — a five-minute window — because the question is "what is happening now," and a rate over all time is a meaningless average that hides the spike. A window is the when of the warrant.
  • Metric scope. One signal, named — orders_api_requests_total, not "every metric matching .*." The query targets the specific series that answers the specific question. Scope is the thing the warrant names.
  • Cardinality limit. Aggregate before you explode. sum(rate(...)) by (le) collapses thousands of per-label time series into the handful you can actually reason about. A query that returns one line per pod per endpoint per status code is a firehose even with a tight window. Aggregation is the place — you look at the garage, not every house on the street.

A query that has all three is a question with an answer you can act on. A query missing any of them is a dump, and a dump is where incidents go to waste an hour. The Northstar recording rules are pre-bounded exactly this way — orders_api:error_rate:5m is a windowed, scoped, aggregated SLI computed continuously — which is why an alert can gate on it and an agent can query it without pulling the firehose.

Hypothesis-driven incident response: form, then try to falsify​

With bounded evidence in hand, the last piece is how you reason toward a cause. The instinct under pressure is to grab the first plausible explanation and start fixing. The discipline is the opposite: form a hypothesis, then spend your evidence trying to break it, not confirm it.

Analogy: a good detective and a bad detective both name a suspect early. The difference is what they do next. The bad detective looks for evidence that fits the suspect and stops when they find some — which is easy, because a busy scene has evidence that fits almost anyone. The good detective takes the leading hypothesis and actively tries to rule it out: "if it was the retry config, then the upstream retry counter should be climbing and CPU should be roughly normal — let me check whether that holds, and if it doesn't, the hypothesis is dead." A hypothesis you only ever try to confirm is a bias with a badge. A hypothesis you try to falsify and fails to break is a conclusion you can act on.

This matters doubly with agents, and there is a specific trap. If you fan an incident out to three investigators — one on the application, one on resources, one on recent changes — the value is that they reason independently. The failure mode is consensus theater: three agents that each read the others' answers and "agree," not because the evidence converged but because they anchored on a peer's conclusion. Three echoes of one guess look like three confirmations and are worth nothing. Northstar's synthesize-hypotheses.sh enforces this structurally — a hypothesis packet that is derived_from a peer's conclusion instead of its own evidence is rejected, and independent packets that converge on the same root cause from their own sources are the only thing that counts as corroboration.

Notice where the loop ends: at an evidence packet, handed to a different role that can act. The investigator diagnoses; it does not fix. Northstar's sre-investigator agent has no Edit or Write tool at all — it produces a structured packet (hypothesis, confidence, sources, and an honest list of what it did not check) and stops. Every claim must trace to a source the agent actually ran or read: no source, no claim. That separation is what keeps a stressed investigator (human or agent) from skipping straight to a fix for a cause they never proved.

The retry storm: why the obvious fix is the wrong one​

The incident this module builds toward is a retry storm, and it earns its own section because it is the canonical example of a cause that hides behind a plausible wrong answer.

The setup: a configuration change raises orders-api's retry count from 3 to 10 and its upstream timeout from 5s to 30s. Meanwhile inventory-api gets slow (an unrelated latency blip). Now every upstream call that would have failed fast instead hangs for up to 30 seconds, and the client retries it up to 11 times with exponential backoff. One slow upstream request becomes eleven slow requests. Latency explodes, workers pile up waiting on retries, 5xx errors climb as requests time out, and — the cruel part — the retries amplify load on the already-slow upstream, making it slower, which triggers more retries. The system attacks itself.

Here is the trap. The dashboard shows high latency and high CPU (workers spinning on retries), so the obvious hypothesis is resource pressure: "we are overloaded, add more CPU / more replicas." It is plausible, it fits the CPU graph, and it is wrong. Scaling up gives the retry storm more workers to consume; it treats a symptom and can make the amplification worse. The causal change was never load — it was the retry configuration. The resource hypothesis is exactly the kind of confident-but-wrong story an undisciplined responder (or agent) produces, and the only thing that kills it is falsification: if it were resource pressure, raising limits would help — but there is no resources block to raise, and the retry counter climbing while traffic stayed flat says the load is self-inflicted, not external. The Deep Dive runs this incident end to end.

The obvious fix during an incident is a hypothesis, not a diagnosis

"Add CPU" during a latency spike feels like action, and action feels like progress. But an unfalsified hypothesis dressed as a fix is how incidents get longer, not shorter — you spend the window on a change that does not address the cause, and the real fault survives it. The habit that saves you: before you propose the fix, state the prediction the fix implies, and check whether the evidence supports it. If "add CPU" is right, saturation is the cause and the retry counter is quiet. If the retry counter is screaming, the fix is somewhere else.

From postmortem to reviewed skill: closing the loop​

The last idea is what you do after the incident, and it is where SRE stops being firefighting and becomes engineering.

Analogy: a postmortem that only produces a document is a lesson written on a whiteboard in a room nobody re-enters. The learning exists, but it does not change what happens next time — the next responder starts from the same blank slate. Converting the postmortem into a reviewed skill is erasing the whiteboard and installing a sign at the actual intersection: the knowledge now lives where the work happens, and it acts on everyone who passes, not just the people who were in the room. When you diagnose a retry storm and write down "here is how you recognize it — retry counter climbing, traffic flat, CPU high but no resources block; here is how you falsify the resource hypothesis; here is the contained repair," that procedure should become a Skill (Module 6's mechanism) that the next investigation loads and follows, not a PDF in a folder.

The word "reviewed" is load-bearing. A skill distilled from an incident encodes a claim about how the system fails, and a wrong claim automated is worse than no claim — it sends the next investigation confidently down a dead end. So the postmortem-to-skill path runs through the same gate as any other change: someone reviews the procedure before it becomes the thing agents run. Toil — the repetitive manual investigation you did by hand this time — becomes a reviewed, reusable procedure, which is how a team gets faster at incidents over time instead of relearning the same storm every quarter.

What you will carry out of this module​

You will have defined orders-api's SLOs and error budget as real Prometheus rules; read the four golden signals as the bounded evidence set; sequenced metrics → events → logs → traces so the expensive tools come last and aimed narrow; written bounded queries that carry a time window, a metric scope, and a cardinality limit; formed hypotheses and tried to falsify them with the sre-investigator agent producing evidence packets that trace every claim to a source; and seen how independent investigators corroborate without consensus theater.

The Deep Dive then hands you the retry storm live. The dashboard will beg you to add CPU. You will falsify the resource hypothesis with bounded evidence, prove the retry configuration is the causal change, and propose the contained repair the only way this course allows — through GitOps, on a branch, for the reconciler to sync, with the investigator that found the fault deliberately unable to apply it.
