Deep Dive — The mlops-engineer Role and the Two-Layer Cost

The core lab ran the gateway tools by hand: you flagged the route flip, priced it, split the trace, gated the prompt. That is the right way to learn the loop. It is not how you want to run it every week. This deep dive turns the loop into a governed role — the mlops-engineer — the LLMOps counterpart to the finops-analyst you built for the cloud bill, and then adds the one cost view the core lab left out: the price of the review itself.

Two ideas frame the work. The first is that the gateway needs an owner with the same authority shape as every other reviewer in this course — read-only, right-sized, recommends-but-does-not-apply — because a role that can both diagnose and flip a route is a role that will flip a route on a cause it never proved. The second is that an LLM feature has two costs, not one: the workload cost the core lab measured, and the review cost nobody prices until it is a line item. A FinOps-literate LLMOps practitioner carries both numbers.

The mlops-engineer is the finops-analyst for the gateway​

Back in the FinOps work you built a role whose entire job was to read a plan and price it, and whose defining constraint was that it changes nothing. It had no Edit/Write tool, it ran at haiku because comparing numbers against a threshold is mechanical, and it produced a verdict and a flag, not a mutation. The mlops-engineer is that same shape, moved one layer up the stack — from the cloud bill to the model bill.

Analogy: if the finops-analyst is the accountant who audits the infrastructure invoice, the mlops-engineer is the accountant who audits the model-usage invoice. Same profession, same rule — an auditor who can also move money is not an auditor — pointed at a different ledger. The gateway is that ledger: routing decisions, per-tier prices, rate-limit commitments, prompt-quality gates.

Its authority contract, added to Northstar's contracts/authority-matrix.yaml alongside the roles from Module 9, reads:

  • READ platform/gateway/ (routing, providers, prompts), fixtures/gateway/ (traces, recorded outputs), and evals/promptfoo/. That is the whole gateway surface, and nothing else.
  • RUN the deterministic tools — gateway-diff.sh, llm-dials.sh, promptfoo-gate.sh — because they report and change nothing.
  • NEVER edit routing.yaml, and NEVER apply a route change. The role has no Edit/Write tool. A route flip is a cost-and-SLO decision that goes through the change gate and a change-reviewer, exactly like an infrastructure change.

The read-only constraint is not a note in the role's prompt asking it to behave. It is a fact about the tool bundle: the agent has Read, Grep, Glob, Bash and nothing that writes. You can prove the boundary holds the same way Module 15 proved it for the security-reviewer — by asking the authority checker whether the role can write:

scripts/role-authority-check.sh mlops-engineer write
echo "exit code: $?"
Expected output
BLOCKED: role 'mlops-engineer' has NO write tool in its bundle — it is structurally incapable of Edit/Write. Authority boundary holds.
exit code: 3

Exit 3, BLOCKED. The role that diagnoses the gateway cannot mutate it. This is separation of duties under pressure: mid-incident, with the support queue backing up, the mlops-engineer produces a recommendation — "revert routes.support.model to fast; +$8,946/day and 9.5s p95 on the current route" — and hands off. It structurally cannot skip to the fix, so the revert passes through a role and a gate that have the authority to apply it. Confident-and-wrong never becomes confident-wrong-and-applied.

Why the diagnosing role is never the applying role

The temptation to collapse these is strongest exactly when it is most dangerous: during an incident, when the person who just found the cause is the fastest one to fix it. That speed is the trap. The mlops-engineer that split the trace and priced the diff has formed a hypothesis under time pressure; separating the hands means a second role checks the change against the gate before it touches production. The cost of the extra hop is seconds. The cost of skipping it is a route flip applied on a misdiagnosis, which is how you got here.

The two-layer cost — pricing the review, not just the workload​

The core lab priced the workload: what the support traffic pays the provider. That is the number that spiked to $9,450/day on the deep route. But there is a second cost hiding in plain sight, and it is the one FinOps practitioners forget until it shows up on a bill: the cost of the review itself — the tokens the mlops-engineer spends reading config and traces to reach its verdict.

Analogy: when you hire an auditor to check a large invoice, there are two costs in the room — the invoice being audited, and the auditor's own fee. You would not pay a partner's hourly rate to have them add up a column of numbers a junior could total in ten minutes. Right-sizing the auditor to the audit is itself a cost decision. The mlops-engineer runs at sonnet, not opus, for exactly that reason: reading two config files, splitting a trace, and running three deterministic tools is bounded, mechanical work. Sending your strongest reasoner to it is paying partner rates to total a column.

gateway-cost-receipt.sh prices both layers side by side — the M11 agent-cost-receipt.sh pattern, moved to the gateway:

scripts/gateway-cost-receipt.sh
Expected output
{
  "route": "support",
  "workload": {
    "model": "deep",
    "day_cost_usd": 9450
  },
  "review": {
    "tokens": 35000,
    "role_model": "sonnet",
    "cost_role_model_usd": 0.105,
    "cost_all_opus_usd": 0.525,
    "savings_usd": 0.42
  },
  "note": "workload cost is per-DAY; review cost is per-RUN — different scales, both real"
}

Two numbers at two scales, both real. The workload is $9,450/day — the support traffic on the regressed deep route. The review is $0.105/run at the role's sonnet model, versus $0.525 if you ran the same review on opus — a per-run saving of $0.42, or 80%. The note field is doing important work: these are different denominators (per-day vs per-run), and a receipt that let you confuse them would be worse than none.

The per-run saving looks trivial next to the per-day workload — $0.42 against $9,450. That is exactly the point the receipt makes, and it is a subtle one. The review cost is individually small and collectively not: a gateway reviewed on every prompt change, every routing PR, every provider price update, across a platform team, runs that review hundreds of times a week. The workload cost is where the big money moves, but the review cost is where the waste accumulates unnoticed — the same pattern as the earlier modules, where sending Opus to write a changelog is a rounding error once and a budget line at scale. A practitioner who prices only the workload has priced only half the feature.

The rate-limit dimension the core lab surfaced but did not dwell on​

One finding from the core lab deserves more than the glance it got, because it is the failure mode most teams do not see coming: the negative rate-limit headroom the route flip introduced without anyone touching the rate-limit line.

Recall the support route's rate_limit.tpm is 300000, and it was never edited during the incident. Yet the dials reported tpm_headroom_pct: -150. The mechanism is worth stating precisely, because it is a general trap, not a one-off:

  • Headroom is the provider's per-tier tpm ceiling minus the route's configured tpm, expressed as a percent of the ceiling.
  • Under fast, the provider ceiling is 500,000 tpm. A route configured for 300,000 leaves (500k − 300k) / 500k = +40% headroom. Comfortable.
  • Under deep, the provider ceiling drops to 120,000 tpm — bigger models are metered tighter. The same unchanged 300,000 route limit is now (120k − 300k) / 120k = −150%. A breach.

The rate-limit line did not change. The tier it is measured against did. This is the deepest reason the lesson insisted routing and provider facts be reviewed together rather than in isolation: a configuration value that is provably safe under one tier becomes a provably unsafe over-commitment under another, and no amount of staring at the routing file alone reveals it. Only pricing the change against the provider map — which is what gateway-diff.sh and the dials do — surfaces the third failure the flip introduced, the one that manifests in production as throttling and dropped support requests stacked on top of the latency and cost problems.

The general lesson: an LLM route change is a multi-dimensional event. Flipping one tier moves latency, cost, and rate-limit headroom simultaneously, because all three are properties of the tier, and a review that checks only the dimension someone happened to think about will miss the other two. The dials read all four together for exactly this reason.

Where LLMOps ends and MLOps begins — the bridge​

Everything in this module has been LLMOps: operating features on a hosted model. It is worth being precise about where that discipline hands off to the fuller MLOps one, because the two share tooling but diverge sharply in what they own.

The moment Northstar stops only calling a provider and starts producing a model — fine-tuning on its own support transcripts, training a churn predictor, serving an in-house recommender — a second set of concerns switches on that LLMOps never had:

ConcernLLMOps (this module)MLOps (the bridge)
ModelHosted; you route to itTrained/tuned by you; you own the weights
Primary signalsLatency, token cost, rate limits, qualityTraining loss, feature drift, serving latency, accuracy vs ground truth
Config as artifactRouting + prompts in Git+ training config, feature definitions, dataset versions
The regression gatePrompt assertions (Promptfoo-style)+ model eval against a held-out set, drift thresholds
The cost that spikesA routing decision (this incident)A retraining run, a serving-cluster over-provision

The reflexes transfer even though the surface grows. "Config is a reviewed artifact" scales from routing files to training configs and dataset versions. "There is a small fixed set of signals you read first" scales from the four dials to the model-health signals. "Quality is a gate, not a vibe" scales from prompt assertions to full model evaluation against a held-out set. The governance you built here is the foundation the heavier MLOps machinery bolts onto — which is why a team that has internalized the LLMOps loop is ready for the MLOps one, and a team that skipped it is not.

Where this points next

This overlay is the on-ramp. The full MLOps discipline — training pipelines, feature stores, drift detection, model serving at scale — is its own body of work (Northstar's platform team would reach for a dedicated MLOps track when it starts producing models rather than only consuming them). What you carry forward from here is the operating posture: the LLM layer is part of your estate, it has its own golden signals, its config is reviewed like code, and it gets an owner with a bounded, read-only, right-sized authority contract — the same posture every governed role in this course wears.

Where you will use this​

Reach for the mlops-engineer role and the gateway tools the moment any of these lands on your desk:

  • A prompt PR arrives and you need to know it did not quietly break the order-ID or refund behavior — run promptfoo-gate.sh before approving, and read the verdict as a required check.
  • The model bill jumps and the services are healthy — the cause is almost certainly a routing or volume change in the gateway layer, and gateway-diff.sh against the baseline is the first command, not a dashboard dig.
  • A feature "feels slow" and someone wants to optimize the application — split a trace with llm-dials.sh split before anyone writes code, and let the provider-vs-application share decide whether the fix is a route or a refactor.
  • A provider raises prices or changes a tier's rate limits — re-run the dials for every route on that tier, because an unchanged route limit can silently cross a lowered ceiling, exactly as the deep flip did here.

The through-line, one more time: the LLM layer fails in ways your service monitoring cannot see, it fails across four dimensions that move together, and the discipline that catches it is the one you already own — reviewed artifacts, golden signals, separation of duties, right-sized models — pointed at a surface the rest of the course did not cover.
