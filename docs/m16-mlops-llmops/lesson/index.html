MLOps and LLMOps

This is an optional overlay

Every earlier module governed the agent doing your DevOps work: planning Terraform, rendering Helm, investigating incidents, staying inside its authority. This module governs a different thing: the LLM-powered features your product ships to customers. If Northstar has a support-drafting assistant, an in-app summarizer, or any feature that calls a model on a user's behalf, that feature is now part of your estate, and it fails in ways your Prometheus dashboard was never built to see. If your team ships no such features yet, you can skip this module and lose nothing downstream. If you do, this is the operational layer you have been missing.

Here is the situation this module closes. Northstar's support team uses an in-app assistant that drafts replies to customer tickets. It has run quietly for months on a fast, cheap model, a few hundred milliseconds per draft, a bill nobody thinks about. Then one afternoon the support queue starts backing up. Agents complain the draft button "spins forever." Finance flags that the model bill for the day is running ten times normal. Nothing is down. Every request succeeds, every draft is fine when it finally arrives. Your existing observability stack, the one you built in Module 14, shows orders-api and inventory-api perfectly healthy, because they are. The thing that broke is not a service you wrote. It is a routing decision: a single line in a config file that quietly moved the entire support workload onto a slower, costlier model, and nothing in your SRE toolkit was pointed at it.

That is the gap this module fills. You are going to learn to treat the LLM layer the way you already treat the rest of the estate: configuration as a reviewed artifact, a small fixed set of signals you read first, a cost you can attribute, and a regression gate that stops a quality drop before it reaches a customer. By the end you will diagnose the support incident from fixtures and traces, prove whether the latency is the provider's or yours, put a dollar figure on the routing change, and deliver the fix through the same gates every other change in this course passes through.

MLOps and LLMOps are two different jobs with two different signal sets​

The first thing to get straight is that "the ML stuff" is not one job. There are two, and confusing them is why teams point the wrong monitoring at the wrong problem.

Analogy: think about the difference between running a bakery and running a coffee shop that buys its pastries from that bakery. The bakery's whole operation is the production: sourcing flour, tuning the ovens, keeping the recipe consistent batch to batch, throwing out the loaves that came out wrong. Its signals are about making the thing: oven temperature, dough hydration, reject rate. The coffee shop makes no pastries. Its operation is entirely about serving what it bought: is the display case stocked, is the price it charges still profitable given what the bakery charges, is the line moving, are customers happy with what they got. Its signals are about delivery and cost, not production. Both are real operations. Neither one's dashboard tells you anything useful about the other's problem.

MLOps is the bakery. It is the discipline of producing and serving your own models: training pipelines, feature stores, model versioning, drift detection, the serving infrastructure that hosts a model you trained. Its signals are training-loss curves, feature drift, model-serving latency, retraining cadence, prediction accuracy against ground truth. If your team fine-tunes models or serves an in-house recommender, this is your world.

LLMOps is the coffee shop. It is the discipline of operating features built on top of hosted large language models you did not train. You call an API. You do not own the model weights, the training, or the serving hardware. Your operation is entirely about the layer around the call: which model you route to, what each call costs, whether you are inside the provider's rate limits, and whether the output still passes your quality bar. Northstar's support assistant is pure LLMOps. Nobody at Northstar trained anything; they wired a prompt to a provider and shipped it.

This module is about LLMOps, the coffee-shop job, because that is what the overwhelming majority of DevOps teams inherit first. You almost never wake up owning a training pipeline. You wake up owning a feature that "just calls the AI," which means you own its latency, its bill, its rate limits, and its output quality, whether or not anyone told you so. The bridge to the full MLOps discipline (training, serving, drift) is where this overlay points at the end; the operational reflexes you build here are the ones you need on day one.

Why this matters for agentic work

Every governance idea from the earlier modules transfers directly. A routing config is a reviewed artifact, exactly like a Terraform plan (Module 11) or a Helm values file (Module 12). The four LLM dials are golden signals, exactly like the four you read first in an incident (Module 14). A prompt regression suite is a CI gate, exactly like the release gates in Module 13. You are not learning a new discipline from scratch. You are pointing the discipline you already have at a surface it was not yet covering.

The model gateway is the reviewed artifact: routing is a decision, not a detail​

Before any dial, there is the thing all the dials measure: the gateway configuration. This is where LLMOps becomes a governance problem instead of a code problem.

Analogy: the gateway is the call-routing switchboard in a support center. A switchboard does not answer calls; it decides which desk each call goes to. Priority customers to the senior team, routine questions to the general queue, overnight tickets to the batch handlers. Changing one routing rule ("send all Tier-1 calls to the premium desk") is a single switch on the board, and it looks trivial. But that one switch can flood an expensive team with high-volume routine traffic, blow the staffing budget, and make everyone wait longer. The switch is small. The decision it encodes is not.

Northstar's gateway sits between every LLM-powered feature and the model providers. Its configuration declares, for each traffic class, which model tier serves it, what the fallback is, and what the rate limits are:

  • support: customer-support drafting. High volume, latency-sensitive (a human is waiting), quality bar is "good enough to draft." This is the class the incident is about.
  • incident: the SRE/agent investigation path. Low volume, quality-critical, latency-tolerant.
  • batch: overnight report generation. No human waiting; cost is the only dial that matters.

Each class points at a model tier (fast, mid, or deep), and a separate provider map resolves each tier to a concrete provider, a price, and a latency profile. The reason to split the routing from the provider map is the same reason you split a Helm values file from the chart: the decision ("support runs on fast") lives in one reviewable place, and the facts ("fast costs $0.80/1M and runs at 900ms p95") live in another. When someone edits the routing, the review can price the decision immediately, because the facts are one lookup away.

The regression at the heart of this module is exactly one line of that routing file. Someone changed the support class from fast to deep. To a plain git diff it is one word. To the workload it is a latency event, a cost event, and, as you will see, a rate-limit event, all at once. A routing change is not a config typo; it is a cost-and-SLO decision, and it must be reviewed like one. That is the first discipline: the gateway config is a reviewed artifact, and a route change gets priced before it merges, not after finance notices.

The four dials are the golden signals of an LLM feature​

An LLM feature can go wrong in a hundred ways, and the temptation, exactly as in a service incident, is to look at everything. The discipline is to not. There is a small, fixed set of signals that tells you whether an LLM feature is in trouble and why, and everything else is a follow-up you earn by reading these first.

Analogy: these are the four gauges on the dashboard, the same idea you used for service golden signals in Module 14, but the gauges read different quantities because the thing you are driving is different. A car shows speed, fuel, temperature, oil. An LLM feature shows latency, token cost, rate limits, quality. Read those four first and the pattern across them points you at a class of cause before you run a single deep query.

DialThe question it answersWhat moves it
LatencyHow long until the user has their answer?Model tier (a bigger model is slower), time-to-first-token, output length
Token costWhat does each call cost, and what is the daily bill?Model tier price × tokens per call × call volume
Rate limitsHow close are we to being throttled by the provider?Configured tpm/rpm vs the provider's ceiling for that tier
QualityIs the output still good enough for its job?Prompt changes, model changes, measured by a regression suite, never guessed

The dials trade off against each other, and that is the whole art of operating an LLM feature. A bigger, deeper model buys you quality and costs you latency and cost and rate-limit headroom. A cheaper model buys speed and thrift at some quality risk. There is no setting that wins on all four; there is only the right setting for this traffic class. The support class wants the fast/cheap corner because a human is waiting and the quality bar is "draftable." The incident class wants the deep/slow corner because getting the answer right matters more than getting it in half a second. The regression flipped support into the incident corner, the exact wrong corner for a high-volume, latency-sensitive, human-in-the-loop workload.

Here is the single most important thing to internalize about the latency dial specifically, because it is where responders waste the most time: when latency spikes, it is either the provider or it is you, and those have opposite fixes.

Provider latency and application latency are different problems with different fixes​

Analogy: imagine a restaurant where a dish is taking far too long to reach the table. There are two completely different causes, and the manager's response depends entirely on which one it is. Either the kitchen is slow (the chef is overwhelmed, the oven is cold, the dish itself takes 40 minutes to cook) or the service is slow (the food is ready and sitting under the heat lamp while the waiter is stuck taking another table's order). Same symptom at the table ("my food is late"), opposite fix. Yell at the chef when the problem is the waiter and you have made nothing better; you have just annoyed a chef who was doing fine.

An LLM request has the same split. The total time the user waits breaks into two parts:

  • Provider latency: the model's own server-side time: how long the provider takes to start streaming tokens (time-to-first-token) and to generate the response. This is the kitchen. You do not control it directly; it is a property of the model tier you routed to. A deeper model is a slower kitchen.
  • Application latency: everything your code adds around the call: queueing the request, assembling the prompt, shaping the response, your own retries and network hops. This is the waiter. You own it completely.

The reason this distinction is the crux of the whole module: the fix is different depending on which half dominates. If provider latency dominates, the answer is a routing change: a smaller/faster model, or a smaller max_tokens. If application latency dominates, the answer is a code change: fix your queueing, cache the prompt assembly, drop a pointless retry. Blame the wrong half and you will spend the incident optimizing code that was never the bottleneck, or swapping models when your own retry loop was the culprit.

A request trace makes the split visible. Each span in the trace is tagged as either provider or application, and summing them tells you which half is the problem. In the support incident, the split will show the provider half utterly dominating, the model is a slower kitchen, which is what proves the fix belongs in the routing file, not the application code. You will run exactly that split in the lab and read the number that settles the argument.

Prompts and model config belong in Git, reviewed like code​

The support assistant has a prompt: the instructions that tell the model how to draft a reply: greet by name, put the resolution first, always include the order ID, never promise a refund the ticket did not confirm. Where does that prompt live?

Analogy: a prompt is a standard operating procedure for a task you have delegated. If your SOP for handling refunds lived as a sticky note on one person's monitor, you would have no idea when it changed, who changed it, or why the refund behavior suddenly shifted last Tuesday. You put SOPs in a controlled document precisely because the behavior they govern is important and the change history is evidence. A prompt is exactly that: a controlled document whose every word shapes what customers receive.

The failure mode when prompts are not in version control is specific and common: the prompt is a string buried in application code, or worse, edited live in a provider's web console, and one day the drafts get subtly worse, a little too eager to promise things, dropping the order ID, and nobody can say what changed or roll it back, because there is nothing to roll back to. The change left no trace.

So the discipline is: the prompt is a file in Git, and a prompt change is a reviewed change, the same as a routing change. It sits next to the routing config, it has a history, and, critically, it has a test suite that runs on every edit. Which brings us to the last dial made into a gate.

A regression suite turns "quality" from a vibe into a gate​

Of the four dials, quality is the treacherous one, because the other three are numbers and quality feels like a judgment call. It is not, or it does not have to be. You make it a number the same way you make any behavior checkable: you pin the behaviors that matter with assertions and run them on every change.

Analogy: this is the taste test on the production line, the same idea as a unit test but for output that is not deterministic. A cereal factory does not ship a batch because the recipe "looks right." It pulls samples and checks fixed criteria: is the sugar content in range, is the weight correct, is the color within tolerance. A prompt regression suite does the same for model output. For the support prompt, the criteria are concrete: does the draft include the order ID? does it avoid promising a refund? is it under the length limit? Each is an assertion. Run them against the model's output, and a prompt or model change that quietly breaks one of them fails the check, before it reaches a customer, not after.

This is the Promptfoo-style pattern (Promptfoo is a widely used open-source tool for exactly this): define test cases with inputs and assertions, run them against the model, score the result, and gate on it in CI. The assertions can be deterministic (output contains the order ID, output does not contain the word "refund", output is under 90 words) or model-graded for fuzzier qualities. The key move is that required assertions fail the build. A prompt change that drops the order ID does not get a warning; it gets a red CI check and does not merge. Quality stops being something you notice in a customer complaint and becomes something the pipeline enforces.

In the lab you will run this gate two ways: against a good set of outputs (every required assertion holds, the gate passes) and against a regressed set (the drafts started inventing refunds and dropping order IDs, required assertions fail, the gate blocks). Seeing the same suite pass and then block is what makes "regression gate" concrete rather than abstract.

Where this leaves you​

Five disciplines, and every one is a governance idea you already own, pointed at a new surface:

  1. The gateway config is a reviewed artifact: a route change is priced as a cost-and-SLO decision before it merges.
  2. The four dials are golden signals: latency, token cost, rate limits, quality, read together, never one alone.
  3. Provider latency is not application latency: split the trace before you assign blame, because the two have opposite fixes.
  4. Prompts and model config live in Git: reviewed like code, with a history you can roll back to.
  5. A regression suite is a CI gate: quality is a checked number, and a required regression blocks the build.

The lab makes all five runnable against Northstar's real gateway config, provider map, and traces. You will diagnose the support incident end to end: flag the route flip, price it, prove the latency is the provider's, and deliver the revert through the gate, and you will do it with deterministic tools that grade the same on any machine, no live provider required. The deep dive then hands you the mlops-engineer role: the LLMOps counterpart to the finops-analyst you built earlier, scoped to the gateway, read-only by construction, and right-sized to sonnet because pricing a diff does not need your strongest reasoner.
