Multi-Agent Orchestration

Module 9 gave you a team of six specialists, the SRE investigator, the IaC engineer, the two reviewers, the platform engineer, the FinOps analyst, each a Role Bundle with a right-sized model, a bounded turn budget, and a structured handoff. You have the players. What you do not yet have is a way to run a play. Right now, if an incident opens, you would talk to one specialist at a time in a single session, threading the whole investigation through one conversation. That works for one bounded job. It falls apart the moment the work has several independent parts that should happen at once, or needs to run unattended overnight, or must be reproducible in CI, or should keep going while you keep talking.

Here is the situation this module closes. Orders are erroring in production. Three things could be causing it, and they are genuinely independent: the application's retry behavior, platform resource pressure, and something in the last few deploys. You want all three investigated now, not one after another while the incident burns. Later that week, a Terraform plan lands that you need audited across four infrastructure modules for security, cost, and policy, and you want that audit to run the same way every time so it can gate a merge. Those two jobs have completely different topologies. Investigating three hypotheses at once while you steer is not the same topology as auditing four modules reproducibly in a pipeline. If you reach for the wrong topology (spawn a subagent where you needed a fork, stand up a multi-agent team where a one-shot workflow would do) you get orchestration chaos: agents duplicating each other's work, agreeing because they saw each other's answers, or spawning until they exhaust a rate limit. This module is about choosing the topology deliberately, before you build it.

Why the topology is the decision​

The reason this matters at work is that the cost of the wrong orchestration pattern is not a syntax error you catch immediately: it is a slow, expensive failure you notice only after it has wasted an incident's worth of time. A subagent you spawned and waited on, when you actually needed three parallel investigators, turned a five-minute fan-out into a fifteen-minute serial slog while production burned. A multi-agent team you stood up for a one-shot audit is now a coordination surface you have to manage and tear down when a one-shot workflow would have been done already. The mechanism was fine; the topology was wrong.

Analogy: think about how an incident commander runs a real bridge call. The commander does not do the investigation themselves. They route it. Some work they hand to one person and wait for the answer before deciding the next step ("go pull the database metrics, tell me what you see"). Some work they fan out to several people at once because it is independent ("you take the app logs, you take the infra, you take the recent deploys, go, all of you, now"). Some work they hand off to run in the background while the call continues ("kick off the full log export, it'll take twenty minutes, ping us when it's done"). And some work is a standing procedure that runs the same way every time without anyone on the call: the nightly backup, the scheduled failover drill. The commander's skill is not doing any one of those jobs; it is knowing which topology each piece of work is and routing it accordingly. Route a parallel job as a serial one and the call runs long. Route a standing procedure as a live improvisation and it is never reproducible. Orchestration topology is exactly this routing decision, applied to agents: the topology of the coordination, chosen to fit the profile of the work.

That is the shift for this module. You stop asking "which agent do I use", Module 9 answered that, and start asking "how should these agents relate to each other for this particular job."

The orchestration decision table​

There are eight topologies worth knowing, and they sort cleanly along a few axes: is a human waiting on the result, are there independent sub-tasks to run at once, must the coordination be reproducible, and does the work outlive the current turn. Read the table as the routing rule an incident commander carries in their head:

The interactive parent: the Ops Lead pattern​

The most common orchestration pattern in day-to-day ops is an interactive parent: a human-facing agent that delegates to specialists, synthesizes what comes back, and never does the specialist work itself. Call it the Ops Lead. It is the incident-commander role turned into an agent pattern.

Analogy: the Ops Lead is the general contractor on a build, not one of the trades. When you renovate a kitchen, the general contractor does not personally lay the tile, wire the outlets, and plumb the sink. They hold the plan, bring in the tiler for the tiling, the electrician for the wiring, the plumber for the plumbing (each a licensed specialist), and they sequence and synthesize: the plumbing has to be roughed in before the tiler closes the wall, the inspection has to pass before the drywall goes up. The contractor's value is entirely in the routing and the synthesis. A contractor who grabs the trowel and starts tiling has stopped being the contractor: now nobody is holding the plan, and the specialist whose job that was is standing idle. The Ops Lead is the same: it holds the incident, delegates each bounded job to the specialist provisioned for it, and combines the structured handoffs into a decision. The moment it starts doing an investigation itself, it has abandoned the one job only it can do.

The Ops Lead's discipline is sequential delegation: hand one bounded job to one specialist, wait for the structured handoff, decide the next step from what came back. This is the subagent topology, used deliberately. It is the right topology when each step depends on the previous one: you cannot decide what to audit until the investigation tells you where the problem is. The dependency is what makes it sequential; if the steps were independent, you would fan them out instead.

Notice what the Ops Lead never does in that diagram: it never edits a file, never runs a fix, never investigates. Every box of real work is a specialist's. The Ops Lead reads structured handoffs and makes routing decisions. That separation is the same safety property from Module 9: the parent that delegates cannot fat-finger a production change because delegating is all it does.

Parallel hypotheses: fan out, then synthesize honestly​

Sequential delegation is right when steps depend on each other. But an incident where three independent causes are possible is the opposite topology: and running three independent investigations one after another is a waste you cannot afford mid-incident. This is where you fan out.

The Northstar incident is the retry storm from INC-002: orders-api latency has spiked to 28 seconds, error rate is over 30%, and three hypotheses are on the table, each in a different dimension:

  • application/retry: the client's retry configuration is amplifying load (an app-layer cause).
  • platform/resource: the pods are starved for CPU/memory and everything is slow (an infra-layer cause).
  • recent-change: a deploy in the last day introduced the regression (a timeline cause).

These are genuinely independent: a different specialist, reading different evidence, could investigate each without waiting on the others. So you fan them out: three investigators, in parallel, each on its own dimension. Then you synthesize. And synthesis is where the real skill (and the real danger) lives.

Analogy: parallel hypotheses done right is a triple-blind study, not a group chat. If you put three doctors in a group chat and ask "what's wrong with this patient," the second doctor sees the first one's guess and anchors on it, the third sees both and goes along, and you end up with one opinion wearing three coats. That is not three confirmations, it is one guess echoed twice, and it looks more certain than a single opinion precisely because it came back three times. A triple-blind study is the fix: each investigator reaches a conclusion from their own evidence without seeing the others', and only then do you compare. If three independent, blinded investigators land on the same cause, that convergence means something, it is real corroboration. If they diverge, the disagreement is signal, telling you the cause is subtler than any single dimension. The whole value of fanning out is independence; the moment the investigators see each other's answers, you have paid for three agents and bought one.

So synthesis has three honest jobs, and each has a failure mode it must guard against:

  1. Corroboration only counts if it is independent. Group the supported hypotheses by root cause. If independent investigators converge, raise confidence. But first verify they were independent: a hypothesis that was derived from a peer's conclusion instead of its own evidence is not a confirmation, it is contamination, and it must be rejected before it inflates your confidence.
  2. Disagreement is surfaced, not averaged. If the investigators split, the synthesis names the split and asks you to resolve it by evidence, not by majority vote. Two dimensions saying "retry storm" and one saying "bad deploy" does not mean "retry storm wins 2–1"; it means you have a real question to resolve.
  3. Missing evidence loops. If any dimension came back inconclusive, or named a gap it did not check, the synthesis withholds a verdict and asks for another pass. Declaring a winner on partial data is how an incident gets misdiagnosed under pressure.

In the lab, synthesize-hypotheses.sh enforces all three deterministically: it rejects consensus theater (a hypothesis derived_from a peer), surfaces disagreement instead of hiding it, and loops on missing evidence: the same fail-closed discipline from Module 8, now applied to the synthesis of a parallel fan-out.

The four anti-patterns​

Every one of the failure modes above is a specific, nameable anti-pattern. These are the four ways orchestration goes wrong, and each has a control:

Consensus theater: agents agree because they saw each other's answers, not because the evidence converged. Three investigators returning the same cause looks like strong confirmation and may be a single anchored guess repeated. Control: keep the fan-out blind (independent evidence per agent), and reject any handoff whose conclusion was derived from a peer's rather than from its own sources.

Hidden assumptions, the parent injects bias into the delegation prompt, so the specialist "discovers" the answer the parent already believed. If the Ops Lead tells the SRE investigator "confirm the retry storm is the cause," it has pre-decided the investigation; the SRE is now gathering support for a conclusion, not testing it. Control: delegate the question, not the answer, "investigate the application-retry dimension and report what the evidence shows," never "confirm X."

Runaway concurrency, too many agents at once, exhausting a rate limit, a token budget, or the machine. Unbounded fan-out is the width version of the runaway loop Module 9 bounded with a turn budget. Control: a concurrency cap, a maximum number of agents in flight at once, so the fan-out has a stopping rule for width the way the turn budget was a stopping rule for depth. In the lab, the audit orchestrator's --max-parallel is exactly this cap, and it must produce the same answer at every width.

Duplication: two agents cover the same ground because the work was not partitioned. If you fan out an audit "across the estate" without dividing it, two workers may both audit the networking module and neither audits storage. Control: fan out over a partition: one module, one worker, no overlap, and assert at the join that every unit was covered exactly once, no repeats.

Read the topology of the controls. Three of the four are structural: they are properties of how you set up the orchestration (blind fan-out, a partition, a cap), not behaviors you hope the agents exhibit. The one that is not (hidden assumptions) is a discipline in how you write the delegation prompt, and it is the one most likely to bite you, because it hides in a helpful-sounding instruction.

The reproducible audit: a workflow, not a conversation​

The second lab job has a different topology entirely. A Terraform plan needs auditing across four infrastructure modules, networking, database, compute, storage, for security exposure and cost blast radius, and this audit needs to run the same way every time so it can gate a merge in CI. That is not an interactive conversation; it is a workflow: a deterministic fan-out over the module partition, one bounded audit per module, joined into one normalized report.

Analogy: the difference between the incident investigation and the audit is the difference between a detective interview and an assembly-line inspection. The detective interview is adaptive, what the witness says changes the next question, and you are steering live. The assembly-line inspection is the opposite: every unit goes through the same stations in the same order, each inspector checks the same things, and the report has the same topology whether you run it today or next Tuesday. You would never run a compliance audit as a detective interview, it has to be reproducible, because its whole purpose is to be a gate you can trust and re-run. The incident investigation is the interview; the estate audit is the inspection line.

The workflow's job is to enforce the three structural controls at once: partition (one module per worker, so no duplication), bounded concurrency (a --max-parallel cap, so no runaway), and a normalized join (every worker emits the same evidence topology, so the report is a merge of comparable records, not a pile of free text). The payoff is determinism: run it at max-parallel 1, 2, or 4 and the answer is identical: concurrency changes the speed, never the result. That property is what makes the workflow CI-able. An orchestration whose answer depends on how many workers happened to run at once is not a gate; it is a coin flip.

In production, each module's worker would be a security-reviewer or finops-analyst subagent from Module 9. The lab's worker is a deterministic read of the repo's own fixtures, the networking module's seven 0.0.0.0/0 CIDRs, the database module's cost spike in the oversize plan, so the workflow is reproducible and gradeable without a model in the loop. The orchestration pattern is identical either way; the workers are swappable.

Where each topology actually lives​

One more thing to hold, because it is where teams get confused: these topologies are not all the same kind of thing. Some are moves you make inside an interactive session; some are how you invoke Claude Code from a script; one is a programmatic library. Sorting them by where they live keeps you from reaching for the wrong one:

  • Inside an interactive session: subagent (delegate and wait, via the Agent tool), fork (/branch, parallel, keep chatting), background session (claude --bg / /bg, non-blocking long work). These are the incident-commander's live moves.
  • From a script or CI: headless (claude -p, one shot; --output-format json for structured output), workflow (a script orchestrating several claude -p invocations, --bare for CI reproducibility), routine (that workflow on a schedule or trigger). These are the reproducible, unattended topologies.
  • As a programmatic library: the Agent SDK, where you write the fan-out in code: several agent calls run concurrently, each with its own system prompt, model, and tool set. The Deep Dive builds the Terraform audit as an SDK script and compares it to the shell workflow on behavior, tokens, and latency.

The SDK is the most powerful and the most explicit: nothing is implicit, you write the fan-out and the join yourself, and you get exact control over concurrency and cost. That is why the Deep Dive goes there: it is the same audit workflow, expressed as code, where every control from this lesson (the partition, the cap, the normalized join) becomes a line you wrote rather than a topology you hoped for.

What you'll do in the lab​

You will orchestrate the two Northstar jobs, the parallel incident investigation and the reproducible estate audit, and prove each control holds:

  1. Run the orchestration decision table: drive orchestration-topology.sh across task profiles and watch it route each to exactly one topology, and fail closed on an ambiguous topology.
  2. Fan out the retry-storm incident: three hypotheses (application/retry, platform/resource, recent-change) investigated in parallel, then synthesized.
  3. Catch consensus theater: feed the synthesizer a packet derived_from a peer and watch it get rejected as contamination, not counted as confirmation.
  4. Surface disagreement and loop on missing evidence: see the synthesis refuse to average a split and refuse to conclude on partial data.
  5. Run the reproducible audit: fan out across the four modules with a concurrency cap, join into one normalized report, and prove the answer is identical at every width.
  6. Prove the structural controls: no duplication (partition + coverage assertion), no runaway (the cap), and fail-closed on a phantom module or a bad cap.
  7. Let the repository certify the orchestration: grade.sh m10 runs the whole decision-table-and-controls matrix.

The Deep Dive then rebuilds the estate audit as an Agent SDK script, the same fan-out expressed in code, and compares the two orchestration styles on behavior, token cost, and latency, so you can see exactly when the programmatic topology earns its extra explicitness over the shell workflow.
